N-of-1 Protocol for COSRX Advanced Snail 92 on Top of a Stable Skincare Routine

Trial core

Protocol synopsis. The objective of this trial is to determine whether continuing COSRX Advanced Snail 92 All In One Cream at the nighttime “trial-layer” step provides measurable additional skin benefit on top of your otherwise stable skincare routine, under a design that is randomized, partially blinded, robust to time trends, and feasible at home. The recommended default is a full-face randomized multiple-crossover switchback trial lasting 74 days total: 14-day run-in/stabilization, then 10 randomized 6-day periods arranged as 5 concealed 2-period blocks, with days 1–2 of each randomized period treated as transition days and excluded from the primary analysis. The two randomized conditions are Snail 92 versus a coded bland hydrating comparator placed in the same position in the nighttime routine. Primary outcome: a daily Morning Barrier Burden Score derived from dryness, tightness, and reverse-coded barrier comfort, measured before morning skincare. Secondary outcomes: individual barrier items, flaking, redness/irritation symptoms, standardized-photo redness metrics, optional moisture-meter readings, acne lesion outcomes, weekly overall skin quality, and exploratory pore/texture outcomes. Safety outcomes: burning, stinging, itching, worsening redness, barrier disruption, eye-area reactions, and acne flares. The default decision rule is conservative: keep Snail 92 only if it shows a practically meaningful and reasonably well-supported benefit over the coded comparator without offsetting harms; if results are inconclusive, the precommitted default is to discontinue Snail 92. This design choice is motivated by core n-of-1 principles: chronic stable question, repeated crossover, randomization, careful outcome definition, explicit handling of carryover/onset, and preplanned analysis. citeturn16view0turn16view1turn16view2turn25search0turn25search6

Causal question and estimands. Your requested conceptual primary estimand is: the incremental effect of Snail 92 when added to your otherwise stable routine, compared with the same routine without Snail 92. That is the most literal answer to “Does Snail 92 provide additional benefit on top of the rest of my routine?” However, for actual trial conduct, I recommend a closely related decision estimand as the operational primary estimand: the average steady-state effect of Snail 92 versus a coded bland comparator layer occupying the same nighttime slot, holding the rest of the routine fixed. I recommend this because your most likely sensitive outcomes are subjective barrier outcomes, and blinding materially improves interpretability when nonspecific effects and expectancy are plausible. The no-layer estimand remains a key secondary policy estimand and should be discussed explicitly in the preregistration. citeturn16view0turn16view1turn25search4turn25search9

The relevant effect is not the same for every outcome. For hydration, dryness, and barrier comfort, the relevant causal effect is mostly immediate to short-lag and therefore best summarized as the average steady-state difference across randomized periods, with explicit protection against the first 1–2 post-switch days. For redness and irritation, the lag is also likely short, though exposure to sun, shaving, cleansing, and retinol may add noise. For acne, lesion development is slower and event counts in your case are low, so the most relevant estimands are distributed-lag or cumulative effects over roughly 1–2 weeks, making acne a secondary or exploratory target rather than a sensible primary endpoint here. For pore appearance and texture, the likely estimand is a noisy, image-dependent average effect over periods, and it should remain exploratory because smartphone-based pore-size inference is fragile. citeturn16view1turn16view2turn16view3turn21search1turn21search6turn10search1

What an at-home n-of-1 can and cannot say should be stated plainly. It can estimate whether the product as used in your routine improves personally relevant outcomes under repeated randomized exposure. It can also test whether Snail 92 appears better than a generic replacement gel-cream. It cannot identify the effect of “snail mucin alone,” because COSRX Snail 92 is not a single-ingredient intervention: the current official ingredient list includes snail secretion filtrate plus betaine, butylene glycol, dimethicone, sodium hyaluronate, allantoin, panthenol, and other formulation components, and COSRX has acknowledged that ingredient listings have changed across packaging/regulatory contexts, so the exact formulation should be verified at trial start. The broader snail-secretion literature suggests possible hydration and barrier-related benefits, but the evidence base is heterogeneous and not strong enough to justify presuming benefit in your specific routine. citeturn13view15turn20view0turn8search1turn8search2turn8search15

Final recommended design. The recommended protocol is a full-face randomized multiple-crossover switchback trial with 10 randomized periods of 6 nights each, preceded by a 14-night run-in. Each 6-night period uses a single full-face condition. The first 2 nights after each switch are treated as transition/washout nights for the primary analysis, leaving 4 analyzable steady-state mornings per period. There is no treatment-free washout period, because explicit washouts lengthen the study, can be clinically inconvenient, and are often inferior to an analytic washout that discards or downweights early post-switch observations. The total planned duration is 74 days, with a prespecified contingency extension of one additional 12-day randomized block if missingness or major deviations invalidate too many periods. The period length is chosen because it is long enough to allow onset/carryover stabilization yet short enough to permit multiple crossovers within <3 months; it also aligns neatly with a fixed every-other-night retinol schedule. citeturn16view1turn16view2turn16view3

This design is preferred over simpler alternatives. A baseline → discontinuation design is maximally ecological but highly vulnerable to regression to the mean, seasonality, retinol adaptation, work/travel changes, and expectation. A simple ABAB withdrawal/rechallenge is stronger than before/after, but predictable alternation is easier to game psychologically and less robust to secular trends. A randomized crossover/switchback is the best default for a short-lag, symptom-heavy skincare question because it balances condition counts over time and supports exact randomization inference. A Snail vs no-Snail design answers the literal added-value question more directly, but because the primary endpoints are subjective and no-layer periods are hard to blind, it is not my recommended default. A Snail vs coded comparator does a better job answering your more specific practical question—“Is keeping this exact product justified, rather than using a generic replacement?”—while still remaining close to your real decision problem. citeturn16view0turn16view1turn25search7turn25search9

The main threats handled by this design are the ones you flagged. Delayed hydration effects are handled by excluding early post-switch days and checking lagged-treatment sensitivity analyses. Retinol-related dryness is handled by fixing retinol frequency and keeping period length compatible with that schedule. Acne lesion latency is handled by demoting acne to secondary/exploratory status and analyzing it with lagged/count methods rather than expecting sharp within-week reversal. Seasonal and calendar effects are controlled by repeated crossover and blocked randomization, though not eliminated. Regression to the mean is reduced by replication. Week-to-week variability is averaged over repeated periods. Low acne event rates mean acne will be low-powered and should not drive your keep/discontinue decision unless harms are obvious. citeturn16view1turn16view2turn16view3turn21search1turn21search6

Comparator strategy. The most literal comparator for your conceptual policy estimand is no Snail 92 and no replacement layer. That contrast is ecologically clean and, because you continue using Lipikar afterward, it is more defensible here than it would be in a minimalist routine: the control condition still contains substantial moisturization and does not collapse into “moisturizer versus no moisturizer.” Still, I do not recommend it as the default because your primary outcomes are heavily symptom-based and expectancy effects matter. A true vehicle-like placebo would be ideal, but for Snail 92 that is not realistically available to consumers: COSRX does not sell a matched vehicle, and the formulation is not just “snail mucin plus nothing.” A generic moisturizer layer or occlusive layer is too active and often too texture-distinct. The best practical compromise is a bland, inexpensive, fragrance-free gel-cream comparator with low irritation risk and no acids, retinoids, benzoyl peroxide, vitamin C, or fragrance. citeturn16view0turn16view1turn13view15

My default comparator recommendation is Neutrogena Hydro Boost Hyaluronic Acid Gel Cream Fragrance Free, decanted into identical opaque containers. It is inexpensive, widely available, fragrance-free, non-comedogenic, dermatologist-tested, and textured closer to a lightweight gel-cream than a heavier lotion. Its official ingredient list is generic-hydration heavy—water, glycerin, dimethicone, olivate emulsifiers, sodium hyaluronate, amino-acid/NMF components, betaine, allantoin, and related excipients—and it is marketed as barrier-supportive and fast-absorbing. That means it is not a placebo. That is acceptable here because the practical question is whether Snail 92 specifically beats a real, low-risk replacement layer. If Snail 92 does not outperform such a comparator, the case for continuing Snail 92 specifically becomes weak. If the comparator is not tolerated in patch testing, the fallback is either a heavier but simpler comparator such as Vanicream Moisturizing Lotion or, if you want maximum ecological relevance over blinding, a protocol amendment to the open-label routine-only control design using the same randomization structure. citeturn26view0turn26view1turn26view2turn13view17

Allocation and routine control

Randomization, blocking, and allocation concealment. Use period-level randomization inside 5 two-period blocks. Within each block, the order is randomized as XY or YX, where X and Y are neutral container codes, not product identities. This guarantees 5 periods of each condition, gives local balance against time trends, and avoids highly predictable strict alternation across the full study. A reproducible seed should be specified in advance and written into the preregistration. Because the participant should not know the future schedule, your friend should reveal only the current period’s code at the start of each new period. This creates proper allocation concealment with respect to future periods, even if partial unblinding occurs from texture or skin feel during the current period. The reasoning follows standard n-of-1 guidance: balanced sequencing is generally more validity-protective than pure simple randomization, while future-sequence concealment and blinding—when feasible—reduce expectancy bias. citeturn16view0turn14view4

# randomization/generate_schedule.R
library(tidyverse)
library(lubridate)

set.seed(20260526)

start_date <- as.Date("2026-06-15")   # replace with actual randomized start
run_in_days <- 14
period_len <- 6
n_blocks <- 5

# Randomize product identity mapping (friend only)
product_map <- sample(c("snail92", "comparator_gel"))
names(product_map) <- c("X", "Y")

# Randomize order within each 2-period block
block_order <- sample(c("XY", "YX"), size = n_blocks, replace = TRUE)

schedule <- tibble(
  block = 1:n_blocks,
  order = block_order
) |>
  mutate(
    p1_code = substr(order, 1, 1),
    p2_code = substr(order, 2, 2)
  ) |>
  pivot_longer(c(p1_code, p2_code),
               names_to = "within_block",
               values_to = "cond_code") |>
  arrange(block, within_block) |>
  mutate(
    period = row_number(),
    period_start = start_date + days((period - 1) * period_len),
    period_end   = period_start + days(period_len - 1),
    transition_day_1 = period_start,
    transition_day_2 = period_start + days(1),
    analyzable_start = period_start + days(2),
    analyzable_end   = period_end
  )

write_csv(schedule, "randomization/schedule_coded.csv")

# Friend stores separately and does NOT share until database lock:
write_lines(
  paste(names(product_map), product_map, sep = " = "),
  "randomization/product_key_friend_only.txt"
)

Operational instructions for your friend. Buy or obtain two identical opaque airless pump containers (or four, if you want backup/overflow) plus two dedicated filling spatulas. Record lot numbers, purchase dates, and photos of original packaging. Fill one coded container with Snail 92 and the other with the comparator. Randomly assign which physical code is X and which is Y; record the key outside your access, ideally both in an encrypted file and in a sealed envelope. Generate the coded schedule with the script above. Provide you only with: period number, calendar dates, and the container code to use. Keep hidden from you until analysis decisions are frozen: the X/Y product identity key, any refills that would expose identity, and the remaining future sequence beyond the current period. Reveal the full key only after the dataset is cleaned, photo processing rules are locked, and the primary analysis script is finalized. This is especially important because n-of-1 guidance explicitly notes that blinding, when used, should be preserved even when adaptation or early stopping becomes necessary. citeturn16view0turn16view3

Blinding and expectancy control. Blinding is realistically partial, not perfect. Snail 92 has a characteristic stringy/tacky gel-cream texture, and a comparator may differ in tackiness, spread, or afterfeel even if visual cues are removed by opaque packaging. Still, decanting both products into identical opaque airless pumps, using the same number of pumps each night, concealing the future sequence, and not disclosing the X/Y identity key all meaningfully reduce visual and anticipatory bias. AHRQ’s n-of-1 guidance is explicit that blinding matters most when one wants to distinguish specific from nonspecific effects; that is exactly the situation here because you care whether Snail 92 itself earns its place. citeturn16view0

Expectancy should be measured, not merely lamented. Each morning, after recording symptoms but before looking at the schedule for the next day, log: perceived_assignment (X / Y / unsure), assignment_confidence (0–100), expect_tomorrow_skin (0–10, where 5 = average, >5 = expect better than usual tomorrow), and belief_product_helping (0–10). In a weekly review, add a free-text note on why you think the period’s product may or may not be helping. If accidental unblinding occurs—because you recognize the feel, see the refill, or are told the identity—record the date, mechanism, and certainty. Continue the trial unless safety requires otherwise, but prespecify sensitivity analyses for pre- and post-unblinding data. citeturn16view0

Routine standardization. The rest of the routine should be standardized enough to protect causal interpretation without changing your real-world regimen so much that the trial answers the wrong question. The morning routine remains: Kikumasamune toner → ZealSea 10% azelaic acid product → Lipikar moisturizer → sunscreen according to the rule below. The nighttime routine becomes: cleanser → Kikumasamune toner → coded trial layer (Snail 92 or comparator) → retinol on fixed schedule → Lipikar moisturizer. No new products, no switching azelaic acid products, no changing moisturizers, no extra masks, acids, peels, pimple patches, spot treatments, or procedural treatments during the randomized phase unless medically necessary. citeturn19search0turn19search2

For retinol, I recommend a fixed every-other-night schedule. Every-third-night would reduce irritation risk further, but it changes your baseline regimen more and weakens the chance of observing the way Snail interacts with a realistic ongoing retinol routine. Adaptive retinol use is the worst choice scientifically because it creates state-dependent confounding: you would skip retinol when dry or irritated, precisely when the primary outcome is bad. Keep the same retinol product throughout. Apply it after the trial-condition layer, not because that is universally the best cosmetic order, but because your explicit question is the effect of Snail 92 as currently used in that slot. There is evidence that moisturization before a retinoid can improve tolerability without necessarily eliminating benefit, which makes preserving the current order reasonable for this personal causal question. If you miss a scheduled retinol night, do not make it up; record the miss and resume the schedule on the next scheduled retinol night. Temporary rescue pauses for safety are allowed and must be logged. Topical retinoids commonly cause dryness, stinging, erythema, and irritation, and sunscreen is routinely recommended alongside them. citeturn4search0turn4search3turn5search2turn5search4

For sunscreen, I recommend an exposure-triggered standardized rule, not a rigid indoors-or-outdoors daily mandate. Use the same sunscreen product throughout the trial. Apply your CeraVe AM SPF 50 in a standardized face dose before any planned daylight outdoor exposure of more than ~10 minutes, ideally in the morning, or before first exposure if plans change later. Record whether you used sunscreen, approximate amount (full dose / partial / none), and outdoor exposure minutes. This preserves ecological validity better than forcing daily sunscreen on true indoor days, while still controlling one of the main sources of day-to-day redness variability. The tradeoff is explicit: daily sunscreen with retinoids is good preventive dermatology, but in this specific n-of-1 it would also introduce a daily moisturizing/filter film that could mask a nighttime-product signal. citeturn4search2turn4search3

For cleansing, use the CeraVe Acne Control Cleanser nightly as the default standardized cleanser, with lukewarm water and a roughly constant contact time. Because the product contains 2% salicylic acid, and because salicylic acid and retinoids can both contribute to barrier symptoms, predefine a rescue cleanser rule rather than switching ad hoc. A rescue substitution with your pre-existing, known-tolerated oil cleanser is allowed only if any of the following are present: morning dryness or tightness ≥7/10, stinging or burning ≥4/10, flaking ≥2/3, or visible barrier compromise. Rescue cleanser use is a planned safety rescue, not a protocol violation, but it must be recorded and handled in sensitivity analyses. Keep azelaic acid, niacinamide-containing products, and Lipikar constant. If you shave, shave on a fixed schedule and only after photos and morning ratings. Change pillowcases on a fixed schedule. Record travel, illness, medication changes, unusual sun/wind exposure, exercise/sweating, mask use, and any accidental product changes. Azelaic acid is generally well tolerated but can also cause mild local irritation, which is another reason to keep it constant rather than adaptive. citeturn19search2turn4search13turn4search19

Outcomes and measurement

Outcome hierarchy. The primary outcome should be a symptom-based barrier endpoint, not acne, because the mechanistic prior for Snail 92 is much stronger for barrier/hydration than for acne, and your acne event rate is too low for comfortable primary-outcome power. This primary measure matches the likely way the product would matter in practice: waking up with skin that feels less dry, less tight, and more comfortable. It is collected before washing and before the morning routine, which keeps it close to the causal window of the nighttime intervention. Patient-centered outcomes remain central in n-of-1 trials even when more instrumented endpoints are available. citeturn16view0

Key secondary outcomes should be: individual MBBS components; daily flaking score; daily subjective redness, burning, stinging, itching, warmth, and irritation; a daily or every-photo-session image-derived redness index from standardized photographs; optional standardized skin hydration meter readings; new inflammatory lesion count; active inflammatory lesion count; weekly overall skin quality; and adverse events. Pore appearance/texture should be explicitly labeled exploratory, ideally using both blinded manual scoring and conservative image texture metrics rather than grand claims about “true pore size.” Acne severity measurement in clinical research commonly relies on lesion counting and global grading, while redness quantification and cutaneous color change can be objectively summarized in color spaces such as CIELAB. citeturn21search1turn21search6turn18view3

A practical effect threshold must be predeclared. My recommended minimal practically important difference for the primary outcome is 0.5 points on the 0–10 MBBS scale, sustained on average across analyzable period days. For redness/irritation NRS outcomes, use the same 0.5-point rule of thumb. For flaking, a sustained 0.3-point difference on the 0–3 scale is practically relevant. For weekly overall skin quality, use 0.7 points on 0–10. For acne, do not precommit to a numeric keep/discontinue threshold, given the expected instability; instead, define harm sensitivity thresholds, such as a visible increase in painful nodules/cysts or a clear increase in outbreak frequency. These are protocol design choices rather than externally validated MCIDs, and should be declared as such. citeturn21search1turn21search6

Home-measurable outcome definitions. Record the following each morning, before cleansing or products. dryness_nrs: 0 = none, 10 = worst you can imagine. tightness_nrs: 0 = none, 10 = worst imaginable tight/pulled feeling. barrier_comfort_nrs: 0 = maximally uncomfortable, 10 = maximally comfortable/settled. flaking_score: 0 = none; 1 = trace flaking in one region only; 2 = obvious flaking in at least one region or visible in standardized photos; 3 = marked or widespread flaking. These are the clinical core of the protocol. Evening ratings can also be recorded, but morning ratings should drive the primary analysis because they sit closest to your overnight treatment window and avoid contamination by daytime sunscreen/outdoor variation. citeturn16view0

For redness/irritation, record: redness_nrs, burning_nrs, stinging_nrs, itching_nrs, warmth_nrs, irritation_nrs, all on 0–10 scales. Record them as subjective clinical state, not as photographic appearance only. Distinguish cosmetic redness from adverse irritation by also asking whether the redness is bothersome, coincides with burning/stinging, or persists >30 minutes after application. Thresholds for concern are given in the safety section. Digital erythema quantification is credible when standardized imaging and color normalization are used; color changes are naturally represented in systems such as CIELAB, where a* captures red–green variation. citeturn18view3turn9search1turn9search6

For acne, define outcomes conservatively. new_inflam_lesions = number of newly noticed papules, pustules, nodules, or cystic lesions that were not present yesterday. active_inflam_lesions = total number of currently active inflammatory lesions. new_comedones may be recorded weekly as exploratory only. Lesion classes: papule = raised inflamed lesion without visible pus; pustule = visible pustular center; comedone = open/closed noninflamed lesion; nodule/cyst = deep, painful or large inflammatory lesion; ambiguous = uncertain lesion type, logged separately and excluded from primary acne counts. Record region by forehead, nose, left cheek, right cheek, chin/jawline. Because FDA acne guidance and acne-methods reviews still treat lesion counts as core endpoints but also warn about variability and outliers, your acne analyses should be descriptive and low-confidence unless a strong signal emerges. citeturn21search1turn21search6turn21search4

For pore appearance and texture, use both visual and numeric restraint. Weekly, after the trial but before revealing the product key, do a blinded manual review of randomized photo pairs and score nose_pore_score, left_cheek_texture_score, right_cheek_texture_score, and forehead_texture_score on a 0–10 scale. In parallel, derive ROI-based texture metrics from the images. Smartphone images can support relative redness and some coarse texture assessment under rigid standardization, but hydration inferred from appearance alone is weak and “pore size” from non-magnified phone photos is particularly fragile. GLCM-based metrics such as contrast, entropy, and homogeneity are legitimate texture summaries, but they should be interpreted as descriptive texture proxies rather than as literal pore histology. citeturn10search1turn10search18turn18view3

Standardized photography protocol. Use your rear camera, not the selfie camera. Mount the phone on a tripod or fixed stand. Use the same room, same lighting, same angle, same distance, same background, and the same time window each session. I recommend buying: a smartphone tripod or mount (budget roughly $15–30 from common smartphone-tripod offerings), an 18% gray card or white/gray/black reference set (roughly $10–15), floor tape or a measuring tape (a few dollars), and identical opaque airless pump containers for the coded products. An 18% gray card is a standard tool for establishing neutral white balance and exposure, and fixed-tripod smartphone setups are widely available. A large lighting purchase is optional; if your indoor lighting is stable, the tripod + gray card matter more than a ring light. citeturn7search0turn7search4turn7search9turn23search10turn23search11

The recommended morning photo workflow is: wake, complete symptom ratings, rinse face gently with lukewarm water only, pat dry, then wait 15 minutes in the same indoor environment with no products applied, no exercise, no shower, and no shaving. Then take photos: frontal, left 45°, right 45°, and optional close-ups of the nose/medial cheek regions. If available, use RAW+JPEG; if not, use the highest-quality default image setting. Disable portrait mode, beauty filters, face retouching, and scene enhancements. If the camera app allows it, lock white balance and exposure; if not, include a gray-card calibration image at the start of each session and hold conditions constant. Digital skin-color measurement is only trustworthy under proper photographic standardization, and custom white balance plus fixed reference cards materially improves color use in analysis. citeturn1search1turn9search17turn7search4

For geometry: mark the floor so the lens-to-face distance is fixed; keep the phone at mid-face height; use the same focal length each time, ideally around a 2x setting if available to reduce wide-angle distortion. Use a neutral facial expression, no smile, mouth gently closed, eyes open, hair off the face, and no glasses. If you shave, do it after the photo session and on a fixed schedule. If you sweat, shower, cry, or spend time in strong sun/wind before the intended photo session, delay or annotate the session. File names should follow a rigid convention such as
YYYY-MM-DD_dayNN_periodPP_codeX_viewF.jpg
with analogous names for L, R, LCLOSE, RCLOSE, plus a metadata CSV linking timestamps, camera settings, and deviation notes. citeturn1search1turn9search0turn7search4

Image-processing and annotation plan. The image pipeline should be explicit and conservative. Import all images and read EXIF metadata. Use the gray card to normalize white balance and exposure across sessions where possible. Convert color to CIELAB and extract L*, a*, and b* summaries from fixed facial ROIs. Predefine ROIs at baseline—forehead, nose, left medial cheek, right medial cheek, chin—and transfer them across days using semi-automated landmark alignment plus manual checking. Store both raw and normalized metrics. Your most credible image-derived endpoint is a relative redness index, based primarily on a* and optionally supported by channel ratios such as R/G. Objective skin color and color change measurement is well grounded in colorimetric research, and digital erythema tools have shown correlation with clinical erythema scoring when images are standardized. citeturn18view3turn9search1turn9search6

For texture, compute conservative ROI metrics such as GLCM contrast, GLCM homogeneity, GLCM entropy, and simple local variance or Laplacian-variance summaries. Treat any pore-like feature detection as exploratory only, and do not let a single image-processing choice determine the substantive conclusion. Manual lesion annotation can be done weekly or at trial end from photos, but because photos are not your sole acne instrument, lesion counts should still rely primarily on diary entries. Add reliability checks by re-annotating approximately 10% of images after a delay and computing agreement statistics. Plan an ROI sensitivity analysis using slightly larger and slightly smaller cheek ROIs. citeturn10search1turn10search18turn9search1

read_img <- function(path) {
  img <- image_read(path)
  arr <- as.integer(image_data(img, channels = "rgb"))
  aperm(arr, c(2, 3, 1)) / 255
}

# Example: gray-card normalization using pre-recorded gray patch coordinates
normalize_rgb <- function(rgb_arr, gray_coords) {
  patch <- rgb_arr[gray_coords$y1:gray_coords$y2, gray_coords$x1:gray_coords$x2, , drop = FALSE]
  patch_mean <- colMeans(matrix(patch, ncol = 3))
  scale_vec <- mean(patch_mean) / patch_mean
  sweep(rgb_arr, 3, scale_vec, `*`) |> pmin(1)
}

rgb_to_lab <- function(rgb_arr) {
  m <- matrix(as.vector(rgb_arr), ncol = 3)
  lab <- farver::convert_colour(m, from = "rgb", to = "lab")
  array(lab, dim = dim(rgb_arr))
}

extract_roi_summary <- function(lab_arr, roi_mask) {
  idx <- which(roi_mask > 0, arr.ind = TRUE)
  vals <- lab_arr[cbind(idx[,1], idx[,2], 1)]
  aval <- lab_arr[cbind(idx[,1], idx[,2], 2)]
  bval <- lab_arr[cbind(idx[,1], idx[,2], 3)]
  tibble(
    L_med = median(vals, na.rm = TRUE),
    a_med = median(aval, na.rm = TRUE),
    b_med = median(bval, na.rm = TRUE)
  )
}

Confounder and diary plan. The key time-varying nuisance variables are: cleanser used, rescue-cleanser use, retinol used, azelaic acid used, trial-condition adherence, trial-condition amount, sunscreen use, outdoor time, shaving, sleep, stress, alcohol, exercise/sweating, travel, illness, medication changes, mask wearing, pillowcase changes, humidity/weather if imported, and unusual exposures such as windburn, prolonged heat, or heavy makeup use. Hold constant wherever feasible: toner, azelaic acid, Lipikar, retinol product, trial-layer amount, morning product order, evening product order, and photo protocol. Safety and data systems

Safety protocol. The run-in period should standardize the rest of the routine, test the data-capture workflow, and confirm tolerability of the comparator. Patch test the comparator on a small jawline or behind-ear area for 3 consecutive nights before randomization. Avoid the immediate eye area throughout the trial. Because topical retinoids, azelaic acid, and salicylic acid can all contribute to local irritation, barrier symptoms should be reviewed daily. Planned safety rescue actions are allowed; the point is not to “win” by enduring dermatitis. Topical retinoid irritation is common and usually manageable, and topical azelaic acid is generally well tolerated but can also cause mild local irritation, so the rescue ladder should be explicit. citeturn4search0turn4search13turn4search19

Use the following prespecified rules. Pause retinol for the next two scheduled doses if either: burning or stinging is ≥5/10 on two consecutive mornings; MBBS components show dryness or tightness ≥8/10 for two consecutive mornings; or flaking reaches 3/3. Use rescue cleanser instead of the salicylic-acid cleanser on any night after a morning meeting the rescue threshold. Stop the coded trial product for the remainder of the current period and contact a dermatologist or other clinician promptly if any of the following occur: hives; swelling; oozing; severe diffuse rash; eye-area swelling; persistent painful burning; or suspected allergic reaction. Stop the entire trial if severe or rapidly worsening symptoms occur, if painful nodulocystic acne clearly flares, or if barrier disruption becomes the dominant signal. Analysis and decisions

Statistical analysis plan. The analysis should begin with visual inspection. Plot daily MBBS, dryness, tightness, comfort, redness, irritation, and lesion counts over time, with shaded bands for coded treatment periods and clear marking of transition days. Next, compute period-level means using only days 3–6 of each 6-day randomized period. The primary confirmatory analysis should be an exact randomization test based on the blocked design: within each 2-period block, compute the difference in mean MBBS between the Snail and comparator periods, then average those five block-specific differences. Under the sharp null, the sign of each block contrast is exchangeable, giving an exact null distribution over 2^5 = 32 assignments. This is assumption-light and directly respects the randomization actually used. citeturn16view2turn16view3

The primary model-based analysis should support, not replace, that randomization test. I recommend a robust Bayesian time-series regression or a frequentist analogue with AR(1) residual structure, Student-t errors if using Bayesian estimation, and predictors for treatment, block, calendar time, day-in-period, retinol the prior night, rescue-cleanser use, sunscreen/outdoor exposure, and optionally expectancy. N-of-1 guidance explicitly notes that repeated observations are autocorrelated and that time trends, seasonal influences, and carryover must be handled analytically when present; distributed-lag approaches are a principled sensitivity check when treatment effects may not be fully immediate. citeturn15view1turn12search15turn12search16

For the primary endpoint, define the estimand as the average steady-state treatment effect on MBBS. The primary dataset uses assigned condition, not actual condition, on analyzable days, because assignment preserves the randomization basis. Nonadherence is then handled by sensitivity analyses: as-treated, adherent-only, and excluding periods with major rescue deviations. Carryover checks should compare the estimated effect when using days 3–6 versus 2–6 versus all days, and also in a distributed-lag model including current-day treatment plus 1- and 2-day lags. If the estimate is stable across these choices, carryover is unlikely to dominate. This follows the standard n-of-1 logic of comparing analyses with and without potentially contaminated early-period observations. citeturn16view2turn14view1

Outcome-specific plans should be prespecified. For MBBS and its components, use daily continuous modeling plus the block-level randomization test. For redness/irritation NRS outcomes, use the same structure. For image-derived redness, model gray-card-normalized a* or a prespecified redness ratio, adjusting for view and ROI; repeat the analysis across a small set of alternative ROI definitions as a sensitivity check. For optional moisture-meter readings, use them only as secondary/exploratory and average replicate readings within site/session, because the literature supports the general utility of hydration devices but also shows that readings are sensitive to site and surface conditions; consumer devices are not equivalent to research-grade corneometry. citeturn18view3turn9search1turn11search10turn17view5turn22search2

For acne, use conservative low-power methods. Analyze new inflammatory lesion count with Poisson or negative binomial regression if feasible, but expect sparse data and many zeros. Also compute a simple outbreak indicator—for example, any day with ≥2 new inflammatory lesions, or any week with ≥3 new inflammatory lesions or ≥1 cystic/nodular lesion—and compare outbreak frequency descriptively. Because acne response is delayed, add an exploratory distributed-lag exposure summary over the prior 7–14 days rather than expecting a same-day effect. If acne outcomes are directionally inconsistent and noisy, they should not override the primary barrier endpoint. citeturn21search1turn21search6

For pore appearance/texture, use only exploratory summaries and insist on consistency across methods before saying anything strong. A plausible rule is that the blog post may discuss a texture effect only if at least two of three sources agree: blinded manual photo scoring, GLCM-based texture metrics, and weekly global skin-quality ratings. Missing data should be handled pragmatically. A period is considered valid for the primary analysis if at least 3 of the 4 analyzable mornings have MBBS recorded. If more than 15% of analyzable days are missing or more than 2 periods are invalid, add one extra concealed randomized block before unblinding. Do not use ad hoc single imputation for the primary randomization test. citeturn16view3turn10search1

library(tidyverse)
library(lubridate)
library(brms)

dat <- read_csv("cleaned_data/daily_clean.csv")

# Exclude transition days
steady <- dat %>%
  filter(period_day >= 3, period_day <= 6)

# Period-level summaries for primary randomization test
period_means <- steady %>%
  group_by(block_num, period_num, assigned_code) %>%
  summarise(mbbs_mean = mean(mbbs, na.rm = TRUE),
            n_days = sum(!is.na(mbbs)),
            .groups = "drop") %>%
  filter(n_days >= 3)

# Join treatment identity AFTER scripts are locked and key is revealed
key <- read_csv("randomization/product_key_revealed.csv")  # columns: assigned_code, treatment
period_means <- period_means %>% left_join(key, by = "assigned_code")

block_diffs <- period_means %>%
  select(block_num, treatment, mbbs_mean) %>%
  pivot_wider(names_from = treatment, values_from = mbbs_mean) %>%
  mutate(diff_snail_minus_comp = snail92 - comparator_gel)

obs_stat <- mean(block_diffs$diff_snail_minus_comp, na.rm = TRUE)

# Exact randomization test over block sign flips
all_signs <- expand.grid(rep(list(c(-1, 1)), nrow(block_diffs))) |> as.matrix()
null_stats <- apply(all_signs, 1, function(s)
  mean(s * abs(block_diffs$diff_snail_minus_comp), na.rm = TRUE)
)

p_two_sided <- mean(abs(null_stats) >= abs(obs_stat))

# Supporting Bayesian daily model
steady2 <- steady %>% left_join(key, by = "assigned_code") %>%
  mutate(
    snail = if_else(treatment == "snail92", 1, 0),
    retinol_prev = lag(retinol_used, default = 0),
    time_num = row_number()
  )

fit <- brm(
  mbbs ~ snail + factor(block_num) + time_num + period_day +
    retinol_prev + rescue_cleanser_binary + outdoor_minutes +
    (1 | week(date)),
  data = steady2,
  family = student(),
  autocor = cor_ar(~ time_num, p = 1),
  chains = 4, iter = 4000, cores = 4
)

Decision rule. Your decision rule should explicitly combine statistical evidence, practical importance, and harm. The outcome hierarchy matters: the primary decision should be driven mainly by the primary barrier endpoint and supported by redness/irritation outcomes, not by fragile pore analytics. I recommend the following categories. Keep Snail 92 if:
1) the primary effect estimate favors Snail 92 over comparator by at least 0.5 MBBS points on average;
2) the evidence is reasonably strong, defined as either exact randomization p ≤ 0.10 or Bayesian Pr(benefit > 0) ≥ 0.95 plus Pr(benefit ≥ 0.5) ≥ 0.80; and
3) there is no meaningful worsening in irritation/redness or clear acne harm.
This is intentionally stricter than “any slight trend,” because Snail 92 is a keep/discontinue product, not a lifesaving therapy. The burden of proof for keeping it should be moderately high. citeturn16view0turn16view3turn12search16

Discontinue Snail 92 if the primary estimate is near zero, clearly harmful, or practically trivial even if statistically suggestive; if the comparator is equal or better; or if there are offsetting harms in redness, irritation, or acne that negate a small comfort gain. Inconclusive but discontinue by default applies when the effect direction is favorable but weak and imprecise, or when measurement noise and partial unblinding leave too much doubt. Run a follow-up no-layer or cheaper-substitute trial if Snail 92 beats the comparator modestly but the advantage looks more like “extra hydration layer” than a uniquely valuable product effect. Investigate a cheaper hydrating substitute if the comparator is non-inferior or better. Exploratory outcomes—especially pore metrics—must not override the primary outcome unless the signal is very large, preponderant across methods, and aligned with blinded photo review. citeturn16view0turn26view1turn13view17

Reporting and execution

Blog-post and preregistration framing. In the preregistration, state plainly that this is a single-person randomized, partially blinded, full-face multiple-crossover skincare trial designed for a personal treatment decision. The plain-language question can be: “Compared with a bland replacement gel in the same nighttime slot, does COSRX Snail 92 make my skin feel measurably less dry/tight and more comfortable, without worsening irritation or breakouts?” Also disclose the conceptual policy question you still care about: “Would I do just as well without this layer altogether?” That honesty about estimand mismatch is a feature, not a bug. CENT reporting guidance exists precisely because n-of-1 trials benefit from explicit statements of design, outcomes, randomization, blinding, deviations, and analysis plans. citeturn25search0turn25search6

Suggested figures for the blog post are: a CONSORT-like single-participant flow diagram; a coded treatment timeline; daily time-series plots for MBBS and key secondary outcomes; period-level mean plots by condition; a posterior or confidence-interval plot for the primary effect; a randomization-inference plot; photo panels showing selected standardized sessions; and a diagram of the image-processing workflow. Suggested tables are: routine-standardization rules; outcome definitions; randomization-schedule summary; deviations and rescue actions; adverse events; and the predeclared decision rule. citeturn25search6turn16view0

The most important limitations should be stated without spin. This is one person. Blinding is partial, not perfect. Home measurements are noisy. Even standardized photos cannot make smartphone imaging equivalent to spectrophotometry or corneometry. Carryover and delayed uptake can never be ruled out completely. Redness visibility can vary with skin tone and lighting. Sunscreen, sun exposure, shaving, and cleanser/retinol interactions remain potential noise sources. Acne will be low-powered because lesion counts are sparse and delayed. Texture/pore metrics are exploratory. The trial is excellent for personal usefulness, but it is not evidence about the product for everyone else. It is, however, very good material for teaching readers about n-of-1 causal inference, measurement design, expectancy, adherence, decision rules under uncertainty, and the difference between “statistically interesting” and “worth keeping in my bathroom.” citeturn0search13turn18view3turn21search6turn16view2
