# Stress Test of the Snail 92 N-of-1 Protocol

This protocol is **not ready as written**. The central problem is not cosmetic; it is causal. The draft explicitly says the conceptual question is whether the participant can remove Snail 92 from the routine, but the randomized primary comparison is Snail 92 versus a **generic hydrating replacement**. Those are different estimands, with different clinical meanings and different decision consequences. The current draft therefore risks producing a careful answer to the wrong question. fileciteturn3file4 citeturn22search0turn22search7turn4search3

Once that mismatch is fixed, the backbone of the protocol is salvageable. The multiple-crossover structure, the attempt to pre-specify outcomes, the concern for carryover, and the desire to separate practical from merely statistical importance are all good instincts. But several secondary problems then become important: concealment is weaker than the draft assumes, rescue actions are not handled as estimand-defining intercurrent events, the measurement plan is overambitious for one person, and the analysis plan leaves too many post hoc forks. fileciteturn3file4 fileciteturn3file1 citeturn7view0turn7view1turn9view3turn26view0

## Major threats to validity

**Highest severity — the design does not estimate the primary decision question.** The draft states that the conceptual primary estimand is the effect of continuing Snail 92 versus the same routine **without** Snail 92, but the operational primary estimand is the effect of Snail 92 versus a **bland hydrating comparator layer** in the same slot. That changes the question from “can I remove this product?” to “does this product beat another moisturizer-like product?” Those are not interchangeable. Estimand frameworks exist precisely to force alignment among the question, comparator, intercurrent events, and analysis. Here, that alignment is broken at the top of the protocol. fileciteturn3file4 fileciteturn3file2 citeturn22search0turn22search7turn4search3

**Very high severity — the comparator is active, not neutral, so a null result cannot justify removal.** The proposed Neutrogena Hydro Boost comparator is described by the manufacturer as a fragrance-free hyaluronic-acid gel cream with natural moisturizing factors that is clinically proven to strengthen the skin’s moisture barrier after one use. In other words, it is not a placebo and not a passive filler; it is an active hydrating/barrier-supportive product. That makes it a reasonable comparator for a **replacement** question, but a poor primary comparator for a **discontinuation** question. A finding of “no difference” between Snail 92 and Hydro Boost would still leave open the possibility that **both** are materially better than omission. fileciteturn3file2 citeturn20view0turn20view1

**Very high severity — the randomization/blinding implementation is weaker than the draft admits.** The posted script is not merely reproducible; it is **reversible**. Because the protocol shows `set.seed(20260526)` and then generates both the `product_map` and the `block_order` in the same public script, anyone with the protocol can rerun it and recover the concealed mapping if the code is used as written. In a self-experiment, that destroys true concealment. The two-period XY/YX block structure also creates local predictability: once the current code is known in the first period of a block, the next period’s code is logically determined. That matters because the primary endpoint is subjective and AHRQ explicitly warns that blinding is especially important in n-of-1 trials with patient-reported outcomes because expectancy can distort reported effects. fileciteturn2file0 fileciteturn3file4 citeturn7view0turn9view0

**High severity — rescue actions are treated like nuisance covariates when they are actually part of the treatment effect pathway.** The draft proposes adjusting for variables such as `rescue_cleanser_binary`, prior-night retinol use, sunscreen/outdoor exposure, and optionally expectancy in the supporting model. That is dangerous. Retinol pauses and rescue cleanser substitutions are **post-treatment events** triggered by symptoms that may themselves be affected by the randomized condition. Under modern estimand language, they are intercurrent events. If the true practical question is “what happens if I keep versus remove Snail 92 in this routine?”, then extra rescues caused by omission are part of the policy effect, not confounding to be adjusted away. Conditioning on them in the primary model would risk post-randomization bias. fileciteturn3file2 fileciteturn3file1 citeturn22search7turn22search16turn4search3

**High severity — the run-in may not actually stabilize what the protocol changes.** If the participant is not already on the exact fixed retinol cadence, cleanser pattern, and sunscreen rule, then the 14-day run-in is probably too short to neutralize adaptation effects. Topical retinoids commonly cause dryness, erythema, stinging, and irritation, especially after schedule changes or initiation, and sunscreen is routinely recommended alongside them. If the randomized phase begins while retinoid tolerance is still evolving, the trial inherits a built-in time trend that repeated crossovers will only partly absorb. fileciteturn3file3 fileciteturn3file4 citeturn1search0turn25search0turn25search15

**Medium-high severity — the measurement package is more ambitious than the underlying instruments justify.** The MBBS is defensible as a pragmatic primary endpoint because n-of-1 trials can center patient-important outcomes, but it is also a composite of highly correlated, expectancy-sensitive sensations. AHRQ notes that composite outcomes can simplify decision-making but can also be harder to interpret and may be driven by the most sensitive component; explicit weighting helps, but it does not validate the scale. The protocol is better on this point than many self-experiments because the weights are at least predeclared, but the result will still live or die mostly on self-report. fileciteturn3file1 citeturn9view1turn7view3

**Medium-high severity — the photo and device plan overpromises, especially for pore, hydration, and redness outcomes.** Standardized photography can support relative erythema tracking, and published ImageJ-style approaches have shown usefulness under controlled imaging. But the literature also stresses fixed distance, dedicated space, standardized lighting, and, for erythema and pigment capture across skin tones, sometimes cross-polarization or other specialized methods. Erythema is less visible in darker skin tones under conventional white-light photography, and skin-color assessment is known to be more difficult across the darker end of the spectrum. Pore analysis is even weaker: published pore-characterization tools typically rely on dedicated imaging systems, specialized optics, or 3D/topographic methods, and even those papers report limitations in reproducibility and only moderate correspondence with visual perception. Smartphone-close-up “pore size” analytics should therefore remain blog garnish, not inferential evidence. fileciteturn1file3 fileciteturn1file4 citeturn3search0turn3search3turn11search1turn11search4turn14search4turn14search5turn14search19

**Medium severity — optional hydration-device readings are currently confounded by the collection protocol itself.** The protocol tells the participant to rinse with water, wait 15 minutes, and then take optional hydration readings. That is a methodological booby trap. Skin-washing and recent water exposure can change barrier-related electrical or TEWL-based measurements, and standardized research protocols typically use controlled room temperature/humidity with acclimatization periods closer to 20–30 minutes. Recent work also reports that washing changed TEWL for about 15 minutes and electrical impedance measures for much longer. So if a home device is used under the current workflow, the reading may reflect the rinse more than the overnight treatment. fileciteturn1file3 citeturn23search1turn23search5turn24search5turn24search6turn10search2

**Medium severity — acne outcomes are not decision-grade under this design.** Acne efficacy trials usually rely on lesion counts together with a global severity assessment over study windows on the order of weeks, not six-day reversible periods. FDA guidance still treats lesion counts and global assessment as complementary endpoints because counts alone miss lesion quality and distribution. Topical retinoid effects on acne also conventionally take weeks, not days. That means acne in this protocol is suitable as a harm-monitoring channel, but not as a serious source of evidence for keeping or discarding Snail 92. fileciteturn3file1 fileciteturn1file3 citeturn5view7turn19search1turn19search7turn1search0

## Safety concerns

The protocol stacks several irritation-capable products on top of each other: a nightly 2% salicylic-acid cleanser, fixed retinol, daily azelaic acid, and then a trial layer whose barrier effects are under investigation. Each of those can be reasonable in ordinary skincare, but in combination they create a high-risk background for barrier disruption, especially if the participant is not already stably tolerating that exact stack. Topical retinoids commonly cause dryness, burning, stinging, erythema, and irritation. Azelaic acid can also cause burning/stinging/tingling, pruritus, scaling/xerosis, and erythema/irritation. The CeraVe cleanser is explicitly a 2% salicylic-acid acne product intended to exfoliate and unclog pores. That makes the background routine more safety-sensitive than the protocol sometimes sounds. fileciteturn3file3 fileciteturn3file2 citeturn1search0turn1search6turn20view2

The sunscreen rule is too permissive for a retinoid-using participant if safety is a serious goal. The protocol deliberately uses sunscreen only before anticipated outdoor exposure of more than about 10 minutes, partly to avoid masking a nighttime signal. That may help sensitivity, but it weakens protection and adds confounding. The 2024 AAD acne guideline states that topical retinoids may cause photosensitivity and that concurrent daily sunscreen use can reduce sunburn risks. FDA consumer guidance recommends broad-spectrum sunscreen and reapplication at least every two hours when exposed. CeraVe’s own SPF 50 product is labeled as a daily morning final step and should be reapplied every two hours for protection. Safety should beat marginal gains in signal detection here. fileciteturn3file3 citeturn16search1turn16search2turn16search3turn20view3

The three-night comparator patch test is too short if the goal is to reduce the risk of delayed allergic contact dermatitis. Repeated open application testing is specifically used because allergic contact dermatitis can emerge only after repeated exposure over several days; some guidance describes twice-daily application for up to two weeks. A three-night jawline or behind-ear test is better than nothing, but it is not a robust exclusion strategy for delayed reactions. If a new comparator is used at all, the trial needs a longer ROAT-style tolerability check. fileciteturn3file2 fileciteturn3file1 citeturn18search0turn18search1turn18search8

The stopping rules are directionally sensible but still too mushy for a skeptical reviewer. “Barrier disruption becomes the dominant signal” is not operational enough. Nor is “painful nodulocystic acne clearly flares.” In self-experimentation, ambiguity at the stop/continue boundary is exactly where rationalization lives. The protocol should define concrete clinician-contact triggers and concrete stop thresholds for persistent moderate dermatitis, eyelid involvement, suspected allergic contact dermatitis, or clustered inflammatory acne worsening. fileciteturn3file2 citeturn1search6turn18search17

## Feasibility failures

The daily burden is too high for one person over roughly 74 days. The current plan asks for morning symptom ratings before washing, a rinse, a wait period, standardized photos in multiple views, optional hydration readings, acne counts, expectancy items, outdoor/sunscreen logs, product/adherence logs, and evening routine data, plus weekly reviews and post-trial image processing. AHRQ’s user guide is blunt on this: measurements should be brief and easy, and the time demands must be realistic. This protocol is not brief, and several of its parts are pretending to be research-lab procedures in a bathroom trial. fileciteturn1file3 fileciteturn1file0 citeturn26view0

The photo plan is especially likely to fail in real life. Four facial views per session for roughly 60 randomized mornings already implies about 240 core images, before close-ups, repeats, or run-in practice shots. That is a lot of files, and the protocol also expects metadata tracking, EXIF handling, ROI definition, normalization, and occasional re-annotation for reliability. The annotation pipeline is not just burdensome; the code skeleton literally says to “replace with preferred package or custom implementation,” which means the most labor-intensive part of the workflow is currently unspecified. That is a strong sign that the glamorous part of the protocol will collapse first. fileciteturn1file3 fileciteturn2file0

Daily acne lesion typing is another likely failure point. Asking one person to distinguish new papules, pustules, comedones, nodules/cysts, ambiguous lesions, and regions every day for months is much less reliable in practice than it looks on paper. Acne outcome reviews already emphasize variability in available scales and lesion assessments. For this trial, that level of granularity is not worth the adherence cost. fileciteturn1file3 citeturn19search2turn5view7

The concealment logistics are also fragile. The plan depends on a friend buying products, decanting them, assigning codes, protecting the key, handling refills, and revealing only the current period code. That is already delicate. Add a public deterministic seed and recognizable product textures, and the “partial blinding” infrastructure becomes mostly theater. If you keep a blinded secondary comparator question, the logistics need to be much simpler and much more robust than they are now. fileciteturn2file0 fileciteturn3file4

## Analysis-plan loopholes

The biggest loophole is analytical **question switching**. The protocol already contains one conceptual primary estimand and one operational primary estimand. That is the original sin. Everything downstream becomes vulnerable once the main question can be reframed after seeing the data as either “Snail versus omission” or “Snail versus replacement.” A preregistration cannot have that much wiggle room and still claim a clean primary analysis. fileciteturn3file4 citeturn22search0turn22search7

The second loophole is **decision-rule pluralism**. The current keep rule allows Snail 92 to survive if either the exact randomization test clears one bar **or** the Bayesian model clears another bar. That is too generous. Two inferential engines with different assumptions are being used as alternative gateways to the same decision. If the result is borderline, that structure invites model shopping rather than disciplined inference. AHRQ explicitly notes that n-of-1 analysis should provide information useful for decisions, not just ritual significance testing, and that statistical presentation should be understandable and preplanned. The current “p-value or posterior” structure is not tight enough. fileciteturn1file1 citeturn9view3turn26view0

The third loophole is **model overgrowth** relative to the actual amount of data. The draft’s supporting model uses Student-t errors, AR(1), block effects, calendar time, period day, prior-night retinol, rescue-cleanser use, outdoor minutes, and a week random effect, with about 40 analyzable mornings if everything goes well. That is too much machinery for too little information, especially when some covariates are post-randomization and some outcomes are subjective. The result is likely to look sophisticated while being unstable. fileciteturn1file1 fileciteturn3file4 citeturn8view4turn9view2turn9view3

The fourth loophole is **underspecified handling of unblinding and adherence**. The protocol measures perceived assignment and confidence, which is good, but it never says what level of successful guessing will trigger an interpretive downgrade. It also defines valid periods based on outcome availability, not clearly on treatment adherence. A period with enough MBBS ratings but weak adherence could still slip into the primary analysis. That is not good enough for a skeptical review. fileciteturn3file4 fileciteturn1file0

The fifth loophole is **open-ended image analysis**. The protocol leaves room to choose among color metrics, ROI variants, redness ratios, texture summaries, manual scores, and “preferred package or custom implementation.” That is far too much analytic freedom for a visually noisy dataset. Published n-of-1 guidance prefers explicit prespecification of weights and methods over post hoc weighing and method selection. The image pipeline, as drafted, is not locked. fileciteturn1file3 citeturn9view1turn9view3

The sixth loophole is **blog-post cherry-picking**. The protocol sensibly proposes time-series plots and uncertainty displays, but it does not pre-specify how representative photos will be selected. In a skincare self-experiment, handpicked before/after photos can overpower the statistics in readers’ minds. If you do not lock a selection rule now, the blog will be vulnerable to the same selective-illustration problem that plagues skincare marketing. fileciteturn1file1 citeturn7view3turn26view0

## Recommended revisions

**Essential — make omission the primary comparator.** Rewrite only the causal-question, comparator, and decision-rule sections so that the primary estimand is the average effect of **continuing Snail 92 versus omitting it with no replacement layer** in the same slot, with the rest of the routine held fixed. That is the user’s actual decision problem. If you want to know whether a generic replacement can substitute for Snail 92, make that a secondary estimand or, better, a **follow-up trial**, not the primary question in this protocol. fileciteturn3file4 fileciteturn3file2 citeturn22search0turn22search7turn4search3

**Essential — stop exposing the randomization algorithm.** Do not preregister a public seed that the participant can run. Instead, have the friend generate the concealed sequence offline and preregister a **timestamped hash** of the schedule plus product key, or otherwise document commitment without revealing the sequence. Also drop the openly described XY/YX two-period blocking if the participant will know the block structure. Use a concealed restricted randomization over all 10 periods with equal treatment counts and a cap on long runs, then base the exact randomization inference on that restricted reference set. This preserves balance without making the next code guessable. fileciteturn2file0 citeturn7view0turn9view0

**Essential — define rescue events as part of the estimand, not as adjust-away covariates.** Choose a treatment-policy estimand for the primary analysis: what happens if Snail 92 is kept versus omitted, allowing the protocol’s predefined rescue actions as needed. Then report rescue cleanser use, retinol pauses, and trial-product stops as **outcomes/context**, not as post-randomization covariates in the primary model. Sensitivity analyses can explore hypothetical no-rescue scenarios, but they should not replace the primary policy effect. fileciteturn3file1 citeturn22search7turn22search16

**Essential — simplify the analysis hierarchy.** Pick **one** primary inferential engine and make everything else supportive. My recommendation is: exact randomization-based block analysis for design-consistent estimation, with a simple pre-specified Bayesian time-series model as sensitivity rather than an alternative gateway. Do not keep the current “p-value or posterior” structure. Also remove post-randomization covariates from the primary model. Keep only treatment, block, calendar time, and perhaps period day if prespecified. fileciteturn1file1 citeturn9view2turn9view3turn7view1

**Essential — reduce the measurement burden to what one person can actually execute.** Keep MBBS as the primary endpoint if you want a symptom-based barrier measure. Keep daily redness/burning/stinging as key secondary harm measures. Keep weekly overall skin quality. Keep acne only as a harm-monitoring channel. Photos should be reduced to a sustainable cadence, such as three standardized mornings per week, unless you are absolutely certain daily imaging will be maintained. Pore metrics and consumer hydration devices should be removed from any decision-driving role. fileciteturn3file1 fileciteturn1file3 citeturn26view0turn14search4turn14search5turn10search2

**Essential — fix the objective-measurement workflow or drop those measures.** If you keep any device-based hydration or barrier reading, do not measure immediately after a rinse. Either collect such readings under a controlled acclimatization protocol before any washing, or drop them. Under the current workflow they are more likely to add pseudo-objectivity than real information. fileciteturn1file3 citeturn23search1turn23search5turn24search5turn24search6

**Strongly recommended — stop changing irritancy-prone background treatments for the sake of the trial.** If the participant is already stably using the retinol cadence, cleanser, and azelaic acid regimen, keep them fixed. If not, do **not** introduce a new retinol cadence or more aggressive cleanser pattern and then call two weeks of run-in “stabilization.” Either extend stabilization materially before randomization or do not make that change at all. A barrier-focused trial cannot afford a moving background of retinoid adaptation. fileciteturn3file3 citeturn1search0turn25search0turn25search15

**Strongly recommended — strengthen the safety rules.** Switch the comparator tolerability check from a three-night patch-style check to a ROAT-style repeated open application test over at least 7–14 days if a new comparator is used. Tighten dermatologist-contact triggers for persistent eczematous rash, eyelid involvement, severe burning/stinging, or suspected allergic contact dermatitis. If the participant remains on retinoid, standardize daily morning sunscreen use rather than an exposure-triggered rule; if you refuse to do that, then image-derived redness should be demoted further because UV exposure will remain undercontrolled. fileciteturn3file2 fileciteturn3file3 citeturn18search0turn18search1turn16search1turn16search3turn20view3

**Strongly recommended — pre-specify adherence, unblinding, and photo-selection thresholds.** A valid period should require both adequate outcome capture and adequate adherence to the assigned condition. The protocol should also say what happens if assignment guessing is consistently above chance in the blinded replacement trial. For the eventual blog post, lock the rule for which photos will be shown before the key is revealed; for example, use the first valid photo session from each analyzable period or a pre-specified random sample of sessions, not “best-looking” images. fileciteturn1file0 fileciteturn3file4 citeturn9view0turn26view0

**Optional — if you still care about replacement, run it as a second trial.** If the omission trial shows that Snail 92 probably matters, then an optional second, shorter blinded trial of **Snail 92 versus generic replacement** becomes highly informative for the cost/substitution question. Do not try to answer omission, replacement, blinding, barrier outcomes, acne, redness, hydration devices, pores, and texture all in one 3‑month protocol. That is how self-experiments become unreadable and underpowered at the same time. fileciteturn3file2 citeturn26view0

## Revised decision rule

Define the primary effect as  
**Δ = mean(MBBS during omission) − mean(MBBS during continuation)**  
using only analyzable days from valid periods, so that **Δ > 0** means omission made morning barrier burden worse and therefore favors **keeping** Snail 92. That aligns the sign of the effect with the actual decision. fileciteturn3file1 fileciteturn3file4

Use the blocked randomized design for the primary analysis, but do not let two different inferential philosophies compete as alternative gateways. A tighter rule is:

Continue Snail 92 **only if all** of the following are met: the estimated primary effect is at least **0.5 MBBS points** in favor of continuation; the exact randomization-based block analysis is directionally consistent and clearly supports benefit under the prespecified randomization scheme; and there is **no countervailing harm**, defined in advance as either a mean worsening of at least **0.5 points** in burning/stinging/redness or any safety-stop event. If these conditions are not all met, the default action is **discontinue Snail 92**. Bayesian modeling can be reported as a sensitivity analysis, but it should not serve as an alternative rescue path for a borderline result. AHRQ’s guidance is consistent with this idea: clinical significance should be set a priori, uncertainty should still matter, and unclear trials may justify extension rather than narrative rationalization. fileciteturn1file1 citeturn26view0turn9view3turn7view1

If you prefer a Bayesian primary rule instead, keep it equally strict and singular: continue Snail 92 only if a pre-specified skeptical model gives a high posterior probability that **Δ ≥ 0.5**, with the exact randomization result reported as a consistency check rather than an alternative basis for continuation. The key point is not the philosophy. The key point is that the rule must have **one primary gateway**, not several. citeturn9view3turn22search0

## Final verdict

**Needs major revision.** The core logic is currently off-target because the randomized comparison does not estimate the main discontinuation question. The concealment plan is also compromised by the public seeded script and partially predictable blocking, the safety background is noisier and riskier than the protocol acknowledges, and the analysis plan contains too many escape hatches. fileciteturn3file4 fileciteturn2file0 citeturn22search7turn9view0turn1search0turn16search3

The good news is that this is fixable without rewriting the project from scratch. If you make omission the primary comparator, conceal the sequence properly, simplify the outcome package, and lock a single decision rule, the protocol becomes a strong and teachable n-of-1 study. As written, though, it is at real risk of producing a polished blog post built on a misaligned estimand. That is not a minor defect. It is the reason the protocol is not yet fit to run. fileciteturn3file4 citeturn7view3turn26view0
